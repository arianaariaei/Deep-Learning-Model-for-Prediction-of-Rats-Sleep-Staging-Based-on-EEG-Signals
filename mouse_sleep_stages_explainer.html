The mouse is awake and active. The EEG is desynchronized — many neurons firing independently, producing high-frequency low-amplitude noise. EMG is strong because muscles are in use. This is the majority class (51%) and also the easiest for the model to classify because the EMG signal alone is a strong indicator.
NREM sleep (code 2) 41.5% of data class 1 in model
Dominant freq.Delta (0.5–4 Hz)
EEG amplitudeVery high — big slow waves
EMGLow — muscles relaxed
EyesClosed, still
The mouse is in deep sleep. Huge numbers of neurons fire together in slow synchronized waves — this is called delta activity. The brain is consolidating memories and clearing metabolic waste. EMG drops near zero because muscles are atonic. In your dataset this is the second-largest class (41.5%) and the most visually distinctive on EEG — the giant slow waves are unmistakable. Delta power is the single most useful feature for your model.
REM sleep (code 3) 5.9% of data class 2 in model minority class
Dominant freq.Theta (4–8 Hz)
EEG amplitudeMedium — irregular mixed
EMGNearly zero — muscle atonia
EyesRapid movement (REM)
This is the dreaming stage. The EEG looks superficially similar to Wake — both have low-amplitude, relatively fast activity — but the EMG is the key differentiator: in REM, muscles are completely paralyzed (atonia) as a safety mechanism to stop the mouse acting out its dreams. The brain is very active (theta waves dominate in mice). This is your hardest class — only 6% of data, and EEG alone can be confused with Wake. Your model must lean heavily on EMG to separate REM from Wake.

These epochs are not real brain signals — they are contamination from the mouse moving around, scratching, or the electrode losing contact with the skull. Human scorers label these separately so they can be excluded. In preprocessing (Step 3), you will filter out all epochs where stage == 4 before building your training set. Never let the model learn from artifacts, as they will confuse every class boundary.
